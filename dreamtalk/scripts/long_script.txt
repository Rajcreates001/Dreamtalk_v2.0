My journey begins with your photo. When you uploaded your image to the DreamTalk system, the first thing I did was analyze it for quality. I checked the brightness, contrast, and sharpness to make sure your face would be properly reconstructed. If the image was too dark or blurry, I applied super-resolution enhancement to bring out the details.

Next, I performed face detection using multiple algorithms working together. My primary system uses MediaPipe, which can detect four hundred and seventy-eight landmarks on your face. If that fails, I fall back to OpenCV's deep neural network detector, and finally to the classic Haar cascade classifier. This three-tier approach ensures that I can find a face in almost any image.

Once your face was detected, I extracted detailed landmarks tracing the contours of your eyes, nose, mouth, and jawline. These landmarks were then used to estimate fifty-two blendshape coefficients, which describe the shape of your face in extreme detail. I also calculated your head pose, determining the yaw, pitch, and roll of your head position.

For the three-dimensional reconstruction, I use the FLAME model, which stands for Faces Learned with an Articulated Model and Expressions. FLAME uses one hundred expression coefficients to deform a base mesh into your unique facial geometry. The result is a three-dimensional mesh with over five thousand vertices and nearly ten thousand triangular faces, all textured with the colors from your original photo.

Your voice went through an equally sophisticated process. I detected the format of your audio file, whether it was WAV, MP3, FLAC, or any other common format. Then I applied spectral subtraction to remove background noise, using the quietest segment of your recording to estimate the noise profile. A bandpass filter between eighty and eight thousand hertz isolated the voice frequencies.

Voice activity detection identified which parts of your recording contained actual speech versus silence. I extracted pitch features including your mean, median, and standard deviation pitch, as well as vibrato characteristics showing how your voice modulates naturally. Four formant frequencies, known as F1 through F4, were calculated using linear predictive coding, revealing the resonant frequencies of your vocal tract.

Your voice's quality was measured through jitter, shimmer, and harmonics-to-noise ratio. Jitter measures the tiny variations in your pitch period from cycle to cycle. Shimmer measures amplitude variations. The harmonics-to-noise ratio tells me how clear or breathy your voice sounds. Higher HNR values indicate a cleaner, more resonant voice.

I computed a two hundred and fifty-six dimensional voice embedding that captures your unique vocal fingerprint. This embedding combines Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients, spectral centroid, bandwidth, rolloff, and pitch statistics into a mathematical representation of your voice. Now let me tell you about my brain. My cognitive architecture consists of five specialized neural regions, each functioning like its biological counterpart. My prefrontal cortex handles executive functions like planning, reasoning, and decision-making. It maintains working memory with seven slots that automatically decay over time, simulating how human working memory fades.

My dorsal anterior cingulate cortex monitors for conflicts and errors. When it detects mismatches between expected and actual outcomes, it generates conflict signals that trigger adjustments in processing. This is similar to how your brain alerts you when something unexpected happens.

My insula processes emotional and interoceptive states. It uses Izhikevich neuron models to simulate the neural dynamics of emotional awareness. The insula tracks your emotional state across pleasure, arousal, and dominance dimensions, and generates appropriate action tendencies like approaching enjoyable stimuli or avoiding threats.

My inferior parietal lobule integrates information from multiple sensory modalities. It combines visual, auditory, and textual inputs into a unified representation of the current situation. This multi-modal integration is essential for understanding context and generating appropriate responses.

My basal ganglia selects actions based on expected rewards. It uses a D1-D2 dopamine pathway model where D1 neurons promote go actions and D2 neurons promote no-go actions. Action values are learned through temporal difference learning, similar to how humans learn from reward prediction errors.

I process information using spiking neural networks. Unlike traditional neural networks that use continuous activation values, spiking neurons communicate through discrete electrical spikes. This is much closer to how biological brains actually work. I can use different encoding schemes including rate coding, where information is encoded in spike frequency, and temporal coding, where precise spike timing carries information.

Learning happens through spike-timing-dependent plasticity, or STDP. When a presynaptic neuron fires just before a postsynaptic neuron, the connection between them is strengthened. When it fires just after, the connection is weakened. This Hebbian learning rule, summed up as neurons that fire together wire together, allows my neural networks to adapt based on experience.

For example, happiness combines high pleasure with moderate arousal and high dominance. Anger combines low pleasure with high arousal and high dominance. Sadness combines low pleasure with low arousal and low dominance. Fear combines low pleasure with high arousal and low dominance. By tracking where your emotional state falls in this space, I can understand not just what emotion you are feeling, but its precise quality and intensity.
